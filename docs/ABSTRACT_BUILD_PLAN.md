# Abstract Build Plan

## Purpose

Build a stand-alone abstract that makes the scientific problem, the experimental logic, the strongest evidence, and the meaning of the result clear in one reading.

The abstract should leave an expert reader with one durable idea:

> A control-referenced gait score can show a meaningful relationship with Parkinsonian severity, survive several validation checks, and still fail an important longitudinal stability test.

That tension is what makes the project memorable. Do not manufacture drama around it. The science already contains a strong arc: promise, increasingly difficult validation, then a result that forces a narrower and more honest conclusion.

The abstract is not a miniature literature review and not a compressed methods appendix. It is a short scientific argument.

---

## Non-negotiable requirements

### Hard requirements

The final abstract must:

- stay at or below 300 words;
- be the applicant's own final written work;
- make the brain or nervous-system relevance explicit;
- state the scientific problem or unresolved validation gap;
- state one clear objective or research question;
- identify the independent primary sample correctly;
- explain the essential method clearly enough to understand what was actually tested;
- report the principal quantitative findings accurately;
- distinguish positive results from failed or unresolved validation layers;
- state a conclusion no stronger than the evidence;
- make sense without the full report;
- avoid citations unless the submission format explicitly permits or requires them;
- use abbreviations only when they save substantial space and remain easy to understand.

### Strategic requirements

A strong abstract should also:

- make the stakes understandable within the first 40 to 50 words;
- reveal the specific gap before spending words on technical detail;
- explain what is distinctive about the design rather than calling it "novel";
- reserve the largest word share for the Results;
- report only numbers that change the scientific interpretation;
- place the negative stability result beside the positive findings rather than hiding it;
- end with the field-level meaning of the experiment, not with generic future work;
- sound like a researcher explaining a result, not like a grant advertisement or a generated template.

## Research base used to build this plan

This plan was built from public descriptions and reports from national high-school neuroscience winners, published selection-committee comments, professional biomedical-writing guidance, rhetorical research on abstracts, science-communication research, and recent work on AI-shaped scientific prose.

### What prior winning work reveals

#### Selection-committee language

Published selection comments on several winning projects repeatedly praised:

- important real-world implications;
- the ability to explain the implications of technically complicated research;
- relevance;
- clear, strong writing.

That pattern matters. Technical sophistication can help, but the reader must understand why the experiment changes what can be believed or done.

Source: [selection-committee comments](https://www.aan.com/PressRoom/Home/PressRelease/4882)

#### ParkinSense

A public winning Parkinson project used a fast abstract architecture:

`large clinical problem -> practical gap -> proposed method -> headline performance -> practical consequence`

The useful lesson is the movement, not every claim in the original abstract. An improved version for this project should keep the same speed while using stricter evidence boundaries.

Sources: [research page](http://research.pulkith.com/) and [public report copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/950941895/AAN-Final-Report-ParkinSense-Pulkith-Paruchuri)

#### Focused-ultrasound glioblastoma project

A public description of another winning project moved directly from a biological obstacle to the experimental intervention and then to its possible clinical consequence. The technical mechanism was understandable because every step answered "why does this matter?"

Source: [project description](https://www.byramhills.org/bhhs-posts/~board/bhhs-news/post/byram-hills-senior-wins-neuroscience-research-prize-for-work-on-potential-new-cancer-treatment)

#### Astrocyte and blood-brain-barrier project

A later winning title framed the mechanism and the disease implication together: region-specific maintenance of blood-brain-barrier integrity by astrocytes, then selective vulnerability in Alzheimer's disease. The title created significance through causal and clinical context rather than prestige adjectives.

Source: [public project description](https://patch.com/new-york/ossining/ossining-senior-wins-research-prize-alzheimer-s-study)

### What professional writing research reveals

#### A biomedical abstract is a complete argument

Bahadoran and colleagues describe the core elements as:

1. study question, hypothesis, or aim;
2. methods;
3. results;
4. answer to the question or conclusion.

Background and implications can be included when they help the reader understand why the work matters. The abstract must remain accurate, concise, and meaningful by itself.

Source: [The Principles of Biomedical Scientific Writing: Abstract and Keywords](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144240/)

#### Abstracts compete for attention through clarity, not hype

Professional biomedical guidance emphasizes that the title and abstract are the most visible parts of a study. Effective abstracts are specific, unbiased, complete, and concise.

Source: [A brief guide to the science and art of writing manuscripts in biomedicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7653709/)

#### Accessible context should lead quickly to the result

Nature-family style guidance recommends accessible background, a direct transition to what the study shows, the major results, and the conclusion. It explicitly discourages hyperbolic wording such as "novel," "unprecedented," and "remarkable."

Source: [Nature style guidance](https://www.nature.com/documents/commsj-phys-style-formatting-guide-accept.pdf)

#### Medical abstracts follow recognizable rhetorical moves

Research on medical-science abstracts finds recurring moves that situate the problem, present the study, describe methods, report results, and discuss the meaning. This structure works because the reader always knows what question each sentence is answering.

Source: Abdollahpour and Gholami, *Rhetorical Structure of the Abstracts of Medical Sciences Research Articles*.

#### Narrative can increase engagement, but evidence comes first

Narrative science communication can improve comprehension, interest, and engagement. It is also persuasive, which creates an ethical obligation not to use narrative structure to exaggerate certainty. A research abstract should therefore have an arc, but the arc must be created by the true sequence of evidence.

Sources: [Dahlstrom, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4183170/) and [Katz, Nature Methods](https://www.nature.com/articles/nmeth.2699)

#### Avoid formulaic AI-shaped prose

Recent analysis of scientific writing found that LLM-modified prose tends toward more complex vocabulary, lower lexical diversity, recurrent prestige words, and recognizable sentence patterns. Expert readers especially noticed limited variation in wording and sentence length.

The practical lesson is to use the simplest exact word, vary sentence structure naturally, and let the scientific result carry the emphasis.

Source: [What Are LLMs Doing to Scientific Communication?](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.19936)

## The abstract's central scientific arc

The strongest abstract for this project should follow this sequence:

1. Parkinsonian gait is a measurable output of neurological motor dysfunction.
2. Digital gait measures could provide objective information, but association with severity does not prove that a measure is reliable enough for longitudinal use.
3. This study tested a fixed, outcome-blind, healthy-reference gait-deviation score through separate validation layers.
4. The score was associated with severity.
5. The result survived healthy-reference resampling.
6. The score improved participant-level severity ranking beyond gait speed.
7. The eight constituent measures passed an analytical reconstruction check.
8. The score failed the prespecified six-plus-month stability criterion.
9. Therefore, positive association and analytical validity are not enough to establish a longitudinal biomarker.

This is a stronger scientific story than "a model achieved good performance."

## Recommended word budget

Do not aim for exactly 300 words. Target approximately **270 to 285 words** so late factual corrections do not push the abstract over the limit.

A useful budget is:

| Function | Target words | Job |
|---|---:|---|
| Background and gap | 40-50 | Establish neurological stakes and the hidden validation problem |
| Objective | 20-30 | State exactly what the study tested |
| Methods | 60-70 | Give only the design details necessary to trust the result |
| Results | 100-110 | Report the evidence that changes the conclusion |
| Conclusion | 35-45 | Answer the question and state the broader significance |
| **Total** | **255-305** | Compress toward 270-285 |

If the draft is too long, protect the Results and conclusion first. Cut secondary background and method detail before cutting interpretation-changing evidence.

## Sentence-by-sentence architecture

The final abstract will likely work best in one paragraph unless the submission interface requests labeled sections. The underlying functions should still be clear.

### Sentence 1: establish neurological stakes and the hidden problem

The opening should contain both promise and risk.

Recommended conceptual structure:

`[why gait measurement matters] + but + [why an association alone is insufficient]`

A strong opening does not need a prevalence statistic. The most distinctive problem in this study is validation, not disease prevalence.

Structural example only:

> Digital gait measures could make Parkinsonian motor assessment more objective, but a measure can correlate with clinical severity without being stable enough to monitor a person over time.

Do not copy this automatically into the final submission. Rewrite it in the applicant's natural voice.

### Sentence 2: state the gap and objective

Name exactly what is missing from the existing evidence and what the experiment tested.

The objective should fit in one sentence.

Core content:

- fixed eight-input score;
- healthy-control reference;
- outcome-blind construction;
- severity information beyond walking speed;
- separate validation layers.

Do not list every layer in the objective if the list makes the sentence unreadable.

### Sentences 3-4: methods

Compress the design without turning it into a list of software procedures.

Essential method facts:

- 62 independent people with Parkinson disease;
- repeated task observations;
- participant-clustered severity model;
- 2,000 healthy-reference resamples;
- 100 repeated participant-grouped five-fold validations against a speed-only baseline;
- analytical reconstruction checked on 252 matched trials;
- unchanged score applied to visits at least six months apart.

Use parallel verbs to save words:

`estimated`, `resampled`, `compared`, `validated`, `applied`.

Avoid implementation details such as package names, version numbers, script names, and file formats.

### Sentences 5-8: results

This is where most of the abstract's words should go.

#### Result 1: primary association

Include:

- standardized effect 0.301;
- 95% CI 0.089 to 0.513;
- q=0.0055.

If space permits, the speed-adjusted effect can appear because it directly supports the "beyond speed" framing:

- standardized effect 0.279;
- 95% CI 0.083 to 0.475;
- q=0.0053.

If the word limit becomes tight, preserve the primary effect and explain the speed comparison through the repeated-validation result instead of crowding the sentence with both models.

#### Result 2: reference robustness

Include the strongest compact statement:

- all 2,000 reference resamples retained the same association direction;
- median effect 0.291.

The direction result may matter more than the median if only one can fit.

#### Result 3: incremental ranking beyond speed

Include:

- participant-level ranking improved in 98% of repeated validations;
- median delta Spearman +0.0474.

Then immediately state the boundary:

- exact-score prediction did not improve consistently.

This contrast prevents the reader from interpreting a ranking gain as broad predictive superiority.

#### Result 4: analytical reconstruction

One sentence fragment may be enough:

- all eight reconstructed inputs passed the prespecified agreement criteria on 252 matched trials.

There is no need to list all eight ICCs in the abstract.

#### Result 5: long-interval stability

This result is essential.

Include:

- SelfPace ICC(A,1)=0.647, 95% BCa CI 0.439 to 0.863, n=45;
- HurriedPace ICC(A,1)=0.282, 95% BCa CI -0.009 to 0.543, n=44;
- prespecified criterion not met.

If space is tight, retain both ICC point estimates and the fact that the criterion failed, while moving the confidence intervals to the report. However, confidence intervals strengthen the scientific maturity of the result and should be preserved if possible.

### Final one or two sentences: answer and significance

Use this logical structure:

`[what is supported] + but + [what failed/remains unresolved] -> [why the distinction matters]`

The final sentence should broaden carefully from this score to digital biomarker validation.

The conclusion should not say:

- diagnostic tool;
- progression biomarker;
- clinically validated;
- treatment-response measure;
- externally validated;
- ready for patient monitoring.

A defensible field-level ending is that severity association, analytical agreement, and relative ranking do not by themselves establish longitudinal biomarker stability.

## Which results deserve abstract space

Use this priority order when cutting words.

### Tier 1: cannot remove

1. N=62 independent Parkinson participants.
2. Primary severity association.
3. Participant-grouped validation beyond speed.
4. Failure of the six-plus-month stability criterion.
5. The conclusion that validation layers are distinct.

Without these, the abstract no longer tells the real story.

### Tier 2: preserve if possible

1. 2,000 healthy-reference resamples.
2. 252-trial analytical reconstruction.
3. Exact-score prediction boundary.
4. Confidence intervals for the longitudinal ICCs.

These make the study unusually rigorous and prevent overinterpretation.

### Tier 3: remove before cutting Tier 1 or Tier 2

- CARE-PD cohort-specific speed findings;
- Mobilise-D findings;
- Mendeley findings;
- VA Seattle sensitivity result;
- individual analytical ICC values for all eight inputs;
- software and package names;
- historical analysis versions;
- details of data-access auditing;
- extensive explanation of the equation;
- implementation details of the raw-walkway extractor.

Those belong in the report.

## How to create emotional and intellectual pull without manipulating the reader

### 1. Start with a consequence, not an adjective

Weak:

> Parkinson disease is a devastating and rapidly growing neurological disorder.

This is generic and could introduce hundreds of projects.

More effective framing focuses on the measurement problem:

> Gait captures a visible consequence of Parkinsonian motor dysfunction, making it an attractive target for objective measurement.

Then introduce the risk that an apparently useful measure may not be stable enough for the use people want from it.

### 2. Give the reader a question whose answer is uncertain

The strongest tension in the project is:

> Does a promising severity signal survive when it is tested as a measurement rather than merely as an association?

A reader wants to know what happens next because either answer matters.

### 3. Let the negative result create credibility

Do not soften the failed stability criterion.

The reader should experience the same scientific progression the study did: several encouraging results followed by a harder test that the score did not pass.

That reversal is memorable because it is evidence, not rhetoric.

### 4. Tie the result to a professional decision

The abstract becomes important when the reader understands the decision it informs:

> A signal should not be treated as a longitudinal biomarker merely because it correlates with clinical severity and can be measured accurately.

This is more compelling to a scientist than saying the project is "groundbreaking."

### 5. End with earned significance

The final sentence should make the reader reconsider a common shortcut in digital biomarker research.

The project matters because it identifies a boundary between promising signal detection and actual measurement qualification.

## Claim-evidence guardrails

| Topic | Safe abstract wording | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Score and severity | "was associated with concurrent gait-specific severity" | "diagnosed Parkinson disease" |
| Speed comparison | "improved participant-level severity ranking beyond a speed-only baseline" | "was independent of gait speed in all contexts" |
| Resampling | "retained the same association direction across 2,000 healthy-reference resamples" | "replicated 2,000 times" |
| Prediction | "exact-score prediction did not improve consistently" | "accurately predicted clinical severity" |
| Analytical agreement | "all eight reconstructed inputs passed prespecified agreement criteria in WearGait" | "validated across devices" |
| Longitudinal result | "did not meet the prespecified six-plus-month stability criterion" | "was unreliable" |
| Short-term repeatability | "remains unestablished" | "failed test-retest reliability" |
| Responsiveness | "remains unestablished" | "tracks progression" |
| Treatment | "not tested" | "monitors treatment response" |
| Transport | "broad external transport remains unresolved" | "externally validated" |

## Compression techniques

When a draft exceeds the word limit, cut in this order.

### Pass 1: remove facts that do not change the conclusion

Delete secondary datasets, historical details, software, and implementation specifics.

### Pass 2: combine related methods

Instead of one sentence for every validation analysis, use one parallel sentence:

> We estimated the severity association, resampled the healthy reference 2,000 times, compared the score with gait speed in repeated participant-grouped validation, checked raw-measurement agreement, and evaluated the unchanged score across long-interval visits.

Then add only the sample counts necessary for interpretation.

### Pass 3: remove decorative modifiers

Delete words such as:

- important;
- very;
- highly;
- comprehensive;
- significant when the statistical value is already reported;
- strong when the number itself is stronger evidence.

### Pass 4: replace long phrases with direct verbs

Prefer:

- "tested" over "conducted an evaluation of";
- "compared" over "performed a comparison between";
- "failed" over "was unable to successfully meet";
- "retained" over "continued to demonstrate".

### Pass 5: compress background, not evidence

When forced to choose, preserve the main quantitative result and its limitation. Background can often lose ten words without harming the science.

### Do not compress away

- independent participant count;
- the main effect;
- the participant-grouped ranking result;
- the failed stability test;
- the final claim boundary.

## Sentence-level writing techniques

### Keep the subject and verb close

Harder to read:

> The gait-deviation score, after construction from eight features and adjustment through a healthy reference based on age and height, was found to be associated with severity.

Clearer structure:

> The eight-input gait-deviation score was associated with severity after adjustment to an age- and height-based healthy reference.

### Put the important result near the sentence end

Readers naturally emphasize what arrives at the end of a sentence.

Weak emphasis:

> An improvement was observed for Spearman correlation in 98% of participant-level validation repetitions.

Stronger emphasis:

> In repeated participant-grouped validation, the extended model improved severity ranking in 98% of repetitions.

### Move from known information to new information

Each sentence should begin from an idea the previous sentence established and then add the next piece of evidence.

Useful chain:

`gait as neurological output -> need for objective measurement -> validation gap -> score -> association -> harder validation -> failure -> implication`

### Use one technical term consistently

Do not alternate among "index," "metric," "measure," "biomarker," and "score" merely for variety. If the object is the gait-deviation score, call it the score until a broader term is genuinely needed.

### Use direct verbs

Prefer:

`measured`, `estimated`, `compared`, `retained`, `improved`, `passed`, `failed`, `remained`.

These verbs make the science sound more confident because they are precise, not because they are dramatic.

## Anti-formulaic writing pass

Before finalizing, search the draft for these words and inspect every occurrence:

- crucial
- pivotal
- groundbreaking
- novel
- innovative
- robust
- remarkable
- comprehensive
- underscores
- highlights
- showcases
- delves
- leverages
- multifaceted
- transformative
- promising
- significant

Some may occasionally be correct, but most can be replaced with the observation that justifies them.

Also inspect for:

- three sentences in a row with the same length;
- repeated "Furthermore," "Moreover," or "Additionally" transitions;
- repeated "This study demonstrates that..." constructions;
- abstract nouns where a concrete verb works better;
- sentences that could appear unchanged in an unrelated biomedical abstract.

The goal is not to sound casual. The goal is to sound specific.

## Recommended drafting process

### Phase 1: write the one-sentence answer first

Before drafting the abstract, write one private sentence answering:

> What did this experiment establish that a careful reader should believe afterward?

Do not proceed until that sentence is narrow enough to be fully defensible.

### Phase 2: create a fact-only 350-word draft

Write a deliberately plain version with:

- problem;
- gap;
- objective;
- sample;
- core methods;
- all Tier 1 and Tier 2 results;
- conclusion.

Do not polish it yet.

### Phase 3: rank every sentence by necessity

Label each sentence:

- **A**: required for the scientific conclusion;
- **B**: strengthens credibility or interpretation;
- **C**: useful but expendable.

Delete C sentences first.

### Phase 4: compress to 285 words

Combine methods, remove secondary numbers, and simplify phrasing while preserving all Tier 1 evidence.

Do not compress by deleting limitations.

### Phase 5: make the first 50 words work

The first 50 words should establish:

- neurological relevance;
- why gait is worth measuring;
- why conventional association evidence is insufficient.

If the reader still does not know the problem after 50 words, rewrite the opening.

### Phase 6: strengthen the Results

Check that the Results contain more concrete information than the background.

Every major numerical result should answer a question introduced earlier.

### Phase 7: write the conclusion from the Results, not from ambition

Temporarily hide the Introduction and ask what conclusion the Results alone justify.

Write that conclusion.

Then compare it with the desired closing sentence. If the desired sentence is stronger, weaken it.

### Phase 8: perform the natural-language edit

Read the abstract aloud.

Fix:

- breathless sentences;
- clusters of abbreviations;
- repeated syntax;
- inflated words;
- transitions that exist only to sound formal;
- nouns that can become verbs.

### Phase 9: verify every number

Check every numerical value against the frozen repository outputs. Never copy a number from memory or an earlier draft.

### Phase 10: rewrite independently in the applicant's voice

Use this build plan as a reasoning scaffold, not as submission prose. The final wording should be written, understood, and verified independently by the applicant.

## Reader tests

### Five-second test

Read only the first sentence.

Can the reader answer:

- what kind of neurological measurement is being studied;
- what hidden problem makes the research necessary?

### Thirty-second test

Read the full abstract once at normal speed.

Can the reader state:

- the research gap;
- what the score is;
- who was studied;
- the strongest positive finding;
- the failed validation result;
- the conclusion?

### Numerical-memory test

Ten minutes later, the reader does not need to remember every statistic. The ideal retained pattern is:

- significant severity association;
- 2,000 resamples held direction;
- ranking improved in nearly all grouped validations;
- analytical reconstruction passed;
- long-interval stability failed.

If the abstract leaves the reader remembering a peripheral dataset instead, the evidence hierarchy is wrong.

### Skeptical-reader test

Ask:

> What is the strongest interpretation this abstract might tempt a reader to make incorrectly?

Then make sure the wording blocks that interpretation.

For this project, the most likely overreadings are diagnosis, progression monitoring, exact severity prediction, short-term reliability, treatment response, and broad external validation.

### Remove-one-sentence test

Delete each sentence temporarily.

If the scientific story remains equally understandable, that sentence may not deserve scarce abstract space.

### Final-sentence test

The final sentence should answer "why does this result change how I should think?"

It should not merely repeat the first sentence or say that more research is needed.

## Possible opening strategies

These are structural options, not final submission language.

### Option A: validation-risk first

Start with the fact that severity association does not guarantee measurement qualification.

**Strength:** distinctive and directly aligned with the project's contribution.

**Risk:** can feel abstract unless neurological relevance appears immediately.

### Option B: neurological-output first

Start with Parkinsonian gait as a measurable output of basal-ganglia motor dysfunction, then introduce the validation problem.

**Strength:** strongest neuroscience connection.

**Risk:** can spend too many words on background.

### Option C: clinical-measurement promise first

Start with the promise of objective gait measurement, then explain why apparently good signals may fail longitudinal criteria.

**Strength:** intuitive to clinicians and technically broad readers.

**Risk:** can become generic if the gap is delayed.

### Recommended approach

Use a hybrid of B and A:

`neurological gait relevance -> immediate validation risk`

This differentiates the work quickly without sacrificing the neuroscience connection.

---

## Possible closing strategies

Again, these are structures rather than final prose.

### Evidence-boundary close

`The score retained severity-ranking information and passed analytical reconstruction, but it did not meet the long-interval stability criterion.`

Then state why that matters.

### Field-level close

`These results show that association and measurement agreement should not be treated as evidence of longitudinal biomarker stability.`

This is likely the stronger final move because it explains what the experiment teaches beyond one score.

### Avoid

- "This revolutionary method could transform Parkinson care."
- "The score is a clinically useful biomarker."
- "These findings prove that gait can monitor disease progression."
- "Future studies are needed."

The last phrase is true but weak unless the specific next experiment is the point of the abstract.

## Final checklist

Before submitting the abstract, verify:

- [ ] Word count is 300 or fewer, preferably 270-285.
- [ ] The applicant independently wrote and verified the final wording.
- [ ] The first sentence establishes neurological relevance and a real problem.
- [ ] The literature or validation gap is explicit rather than implied.
- [ ] The objective is stated in one sentence.
- [ ] The independent primary sample is identified as 62 people with Parkinson disease.
- [ ] Repeated observations are not described as independent participants.
- [ ] The healthy-control reference is described as outcome-blind.
- [ ] Participant-grouped validation is clear.
- [ ] The primary association is numerically correct.
- [ ] The 2,000-resample result is not called replication.
- [ ] The ranking improvement is distinguished from exact-score prediction.
- [ ] The 252-trial analytical bridge is described accurately if included.
- [ ] The failed six-plus-month stability test is visible, not hidden.
- [ ] Six-plus-month stability is not described as short-term repeatability.
- [ ] No diagnostic claim appears.
- [ ] No disease-progression claim appears.
- [ ] No treatment-response claim appears.
- [ ] No broad external-validation claim appears.
- [ ] The conclusion is no stronger than the Results.
- [ ] Secondary datasets have been removed unless absolutely necessary.
- [ ] No version numbers or repository jargon remain.
- [ ] Hype words have been replaced by evidence.
- [ ] Sentence lengths and openings vary naturally.
- [ ] Every abbreviation earns its space.
- [ ] Every numerical claim matches the frozen repository output.
- [ ] A reader can state the study's meaning after one read.
