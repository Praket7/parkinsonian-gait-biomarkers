# Research Report Build Plan

## Purpose

Build a research report that an expert reader can understand, trust, and remember after one sitting.

The report should make one scientific idea unavoidable:

> A gait measure can show a convincing relationship with Parkinsonian severity and still fail an important test required for longitudinal biomarker use.

That is the intellectual center of the project. The report should not read like a catalog of analyses. Every section, figure, statistic, and limitation should advance that central argument.

The goal is not to manufacture emotion. The strongest emotional effect comes from stakes that are already real. Parkinsonian gait affects mobility and independence. Digital measures could make motor assessment more objective and scalable. At the same time, an apparently promising measure can create false confidence if association is mistaken for reliability, responsiveness, or transportability. Let the human significance come from that tension rather than from sentimental language.

---

## Non-negotiable requirements

### Hard requirements

The final report must:

- present original research in the applicant's own final prose;
- make the neurological connection explicit rather than assuming the reader will infer it;
- state a clear research question or falsifiable hypothesis;
- explain a feasible methodology clearly enough for an expert to evaluate it;
- distinguish independent participants from repeated observations;
- report the principal quantitative results with sample sizes, uncertainty, and the correct statistical interpretation;
- place the significance of the findings in proper perspective;
- discuss methodological pitfalls and limitations directly;
- avoid diagnostic, causal, treatment-response, progression, or clinical-utility claims that the data do not support;
- be organized and readable;
- use clearly labeled, legible figures and tables;
- include a bibliography with sources that actually support the statements attached to them;
- maintain numerical consistency with the frozen repository outputs.

### Strategic requirements

A strong report should also:

- let a neurologist who is not a gait-statistics specialist understand the problem within the first page;
- make the research gap explicit before introducing the score;
- state the project's actual contribution before the Methods section begins;
- lead each Results subsection with the scientific answer, then give the evidence;
- make the failed long-interval stability test a central result rather than hiding it;
- distinguish ranking performance from exact-score prediction;
- distinguish analytical measurement agreement from clinical validation;
- separate external supporting evidence from full replication;
- make every important claim traceable to a result, figure, table, or citation;
- end by identifying the next experiment that would genuinely change the evidentiary status of the score.

## Research base used to build this plan

This plan was built after reviewing public descriptions of multiple national high-school neuroscience winners, a publicly available 27-page winning Parkinson research report, selection-committee comments on prior winners, professional biomedical writing guidance, rhetorical research on scientific abstracts and introductions, science-communication research, and recent research on AI-shaped scientific prose.

### Winner and finalist case studies

#### Selection-committee comments on four 2021 winning projects

The committee's published comments repeatedly rewarded three things: clear real-world implications, the ability to explain the implications of technically advanced work, and writing that was relevant and readable. The projects ranged from addiction biology to glioblastoma imaging, Alzheimer-related machine learning, and neuroprotective compounds, yet the praise focused less on technical flash than on why the result mattered and how clearly that meaning was communicated.

Source: [selection-committee comments](https://www.aan.com/PressRoom/Home/PressRelease/4882)

#### Pulkith Paruchuri, ParkinSense

A public version of the report is 27 pages and uses a recognizable professional structure: abstract, introduction, related work, data sources, methodologies, results and discussion, conclusion, error analysis, future work, major contributions, references, and appendices. Its abstract opens with disease burden and access problems, quickly introduces the solution, gives headline numerical performance, and ends with the practical consequence.

The useful lesson is the architecture, not the strength of every claim. A modern version should preserve the fast movement from problem to method to numbers to meaning while applying stricter claim boundaries.

Sources: [research page](http://research.pulkith.com/) and [public report copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/950941895/AAN-Final-Report-ParkinSense-Pulkith-Paruchuri)

#### Samantha Schaevitz, focused ultrasound and glioblastoma

The public project description explains an advanced mechanism through a direct chain: blood-brain-barrier limitation, focused ultrasound with microbubbles, increased drug penetration, then the possible consequence of lower therapeutic doses and fewer side effects. The mechanism is never left disconnected from the patient-level reason it matters.

Source: [project description](https://www.byramhills.org/bhhs-posts/~board/bhhs-news/post/byram-hills-senior-wins-neuroscience-research-prize-for-work-on-potential-new-cancer-treatment)

#### Sofia Magalhaes, astrocytes and regional blood-brain-barrier integrity

The project title itself demonstrates an effective significance structure. It identifies the mechanism first and then names the neurological consequence: region-specific blood-brain-barrier maintenance and selective vulnerability in Alzheimer's disease. The title does not depend on hype words to signal importance.

Source: [public project description](https://patch.com/new-york/ossining/ossining-senior-wins-research-prize-alzheimer-s-study)

### Professional scientific-writing research

#### Manuscript "bookends"

Sefcik, Glasofer, and Smith describe the Introduction and Discussion as the bookends that frame the entire study. The Introduction should move from the problem to what is known, what is unknown, and the present study. The Discussion should return to the study purpose, interpret the strongest results, place them against prior work, discuss limitations, and end with implications.

Source: [Writing Manuscript "Bookends": Strategies to Effectively Frame Science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12459627/)

#### Biomedical manuscript guidance

Forero, López-León, and Perry recommend concise titles, abstracts that are specific and honest, short introductions built around what is known, what is unknown, and what the study adds, and figures/tables that are treated as part of the scientific communication rather than decoration.

Source: [A brief guide to the science and art of writing manuscripts in biomedicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7653709/)

#### Abstract construction

Bahadoran and colleagues describe the abstract as a stand-alone scientific argument built from the research question, methods, results, and answer to the question, with background and implications added only when they earn their space.

Source: [The Principles of Biomedical Scientific Writing: Abstract and Keywords](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144240/)

#### Professional abstract style

Nature-family guidance emphasizes accessible background, a direct transition to what the study shows, the major results and conclusions, and avoidance of hyperbolic words such as "novel," "unprecedented," or "remarkable."

Source: [Nature style guidance](https://www.nature.com/documents/commsj-phys-style-formatting-guide-accept.pdf)

#### Research-space structure

The Create-a-Research-Space model describes three moves that appear repeatedly in successful research introductions: establish the scientific territory, establish the unresolved niche or gap, then occupy that niche with the present study. The important lesson is not to spend half the introduction proving that Parkinson disease is important. The report must reach the exact unresolved validation problem quickly.

Source: [CARS model summary](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/CARS)

#### Reader expectations

Gopen and Swan's classic work argues that scientific clarity depends on reader expectations, not merely shorter sentences. Subjects and verbs should stay close, familiar context should usually precede new information, and the end of a sentence should carry information that deserves emphasis. These principles are especially useful when presenting statistics.

Source: [The Science of Scientific Writing](https://sci.utah.edu/~macleod/writing/sciwriting-gopen-swan.pdf)

#### Narrative and science communication

Dahlstrom's review finds that narrative forms can increase comprehension, interest, and engagement, but also warns that narrative is persuasive and therefore creates ethical responsibilities. Katz makes the complementary argument that scientists should not force messy evidence into a falsely complete story. The practical rule for this report is simple: use narrative structure to organize genuine evidence, never to change what the evidence says.

Sources: [Dahlstrom, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4183170/) and [Katz, Nature Methods](https://www.nature.com/articles/nmeth.2699)

#### Claim-evidence and figure alignment

Recent work on structured scientific manuscript generation identifies common failures in long papers: section drift, unsupported contribution claims, figures that are disconnected from the text, and contradictions between methods and experiments. A useful safeguard is to maintain a claim-evidence map throughout drafting and require every major figure to answer a specific research question.

Source: [Story2Proposal](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.27065)

#### Avoiding formulaic AI-shaped prose

A 2026 analysis of more than 37,000 scientific papers and controlled human/LLM rewrites found recurring shifts toward longer and more complex words, lower lexical diversity, and recognizable stock vocabulary in LLM-modified text. Expert readers also noticed limited variation in wording and sentence length. The implication here is not to make the prose artificially casual. It is to prefer the simplest precise word, vary rhythm naturally, and let the scientific content create emphasis.

Source: [What Are LLMs Doing to Scientific Communication?](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.19936)

## The central narrative for this project

### The one-sentence scientific contribution

Before drafting full prose, lock one sentence that every section must support:

> This study tests whether a fixed, healthy-reference gait-deviation score that is associated with Parkinsonian gait severity can survive the additional validation layers required before it can be treated as a dependable biomarker measure.

The final wording can change, but the logic should not.

### The scientific tension

The report has a natural sequence:

1. Parkinsonian motor dysfunction produces measurable changes in gait.
2. Digital gait measures therefore appear promising as objective clinical outcomes.
3. A severity association is only one property of a useful biomarker.
4. The control-referenced score shows a significant association and survives several robustness checks.
5. It improves relative severity ranking beyond gait speed in repeated participant-grouped validation.
6. Its eight inputs can be reconstructed from raw walkway records with strong analytical agreement.
7. The same frozen score then fails the prespecified six-plus-month stability criterion.
8. Therefore, a positive cross-sectional signal and strong analytical validation do not by themselves qualify a measure for longitudinal use.

That sequence is the report's story. Do not interrupt it with every secondary dataset or every historical version of the analysis.

### The human significance

Do not add a personal anecdote unless it is genuinely part of the researcher's reason for pursuing the work.

Instead, make the stakes concrete:

- gait impairment is a visible output of neurological motor dysfunction;
- clinical ratings are useful but intermittent and partly observer-dependent;
- objective gait measures could help quantify motor state more consistently;
- if a digital measure is unstable or context-dependent, treating it as a monitoring biomarker can create misleading conclusions;
- therefore, proving where a measure fails is clinically meaningful even when it is less glamorous than reporting a new high accuracy score.

The emotional effect should be respect for the problem and confidence in the researcher's honesty, not pity or hype.

## Recommended report architecture

The exact page limit should follow the submission portal if one is imposed. If no strict report length is specified, optimize for a report that can be read carefully in one sitting. Do not add pages simply because space is available.

### Title

The title should communicate the scientific conflict or contribution in roughly one line.

A strong title should contain:

- the biological or clinical object: Parkinsonian gait;
- the measurement idea: control-referenced gait deviation or digital gait measure;
- the actual contribution: validation, longitudinal behavior, or the distinction between association and qualification.

Avoid titles dominated by software names, version numbers, unexplained acronyms, or claims such as "novel," "revolutionary," "first," or "clinically validated."

A useful title pattern is:

`[Scientific tension or result]: [specific system or validation question]`

The title should make the reader curious about the answer, but it must remain literally true.

## 1. Introduction

### Job of the section

By the end of the Introduction, the reader should be able to answer four questions:

1. Why is Parkinsonian gait neurologically meaningful?
2. Why are digital gait measures attractive?
3. What important validation problem remains unresolved?
4. What exactly did this study test?

### Paragraph 1: neurological and clinical stakes

Open with the nervous-system mechanism, not a generic sentence about how common Parkinson disease is.

Explain the chain concisely:

`Nigrostriatal dopaminergic loss -> altered basal-ganglia motor circuitry -> impaired movement scaling and automaticity -> measurable changes in pace, stride, timing, and variability.`

Then connect this physiology to why gait is worth quantifying.

A prevalence or burden statistic can appear, but it should support the scientific problem rather than replace it.

### Paragraph 2: what the field can already measure

Explain that instrumented walkways, wearables, video, and other digital systems can quantify gait features. Establish that many features correlate with disease state or clinical ratings.

Then introduce the hidden problem: correlation does not answer whether a measure is technically reproducible, independent of simple walking speed, stable enough for longitudinal use, responsive to clinical change, or transferable across devices and sites.

### Paragraph 3: the exact gap

This is the most important paragraph in the Introduction.

State the methodological gap directly. Recent reviews of Parkinson digital outcomes continue to identify inconsistent validation, limited external testing, and insufficient longitudinal evidence. The field often has evidence that a signal exists before it has evidence that the signal behaves like a qualified longitudinal measure.

Do not claim nobody has ever evaluated gait reliability or external validity. The gap is narrower and more defensible: these validation properties are often studied separately, with different measures and datasets, and a positive result in one layer is easily overinterpreted as support for another.

### Paragraph 4: question, hypothesis, and contribution

State one research question and one falsifiable hypothesis.

A strong formulation should say that the study tests a fixed, outcome-blind score across separate evidence layers rather than optimizing a model until it performs well.

End the Introduction by naming the contribution in plain language:

- a frozen control-referenced score;
- explicit comparison against gait speed;
- participant-grouped validation;
- analytical reconstruction before longitudinal application;
- a prespecified longitudinal criterion that was allowed to fail.

Do not list every secondary dataset here.

## 2. Methods

### Job of the section

Convince the reader that the result follows from a deliberate design rather than from trying many analyses until something worked.

The Methods should answer both **what was done** and **why each validation layer was necessary**.

### 2.1 Study design overview

Begin with a single diagram showing the evidence ladder and the order of analysis.

Recommended flow:

`Healthy reference -> frozen score -> severity association -> reference resampling -> comparison with gait speed -> raw measurement bridge -> long-interval stability -> external evidence boundaries`

This figure should appear before dense statistical details because it gives the reader a mental map for the entire report.

### 2.2 Data sources and roles

Use a compact table with columns such as:

| Dataset | Independent participants | Measurement system | Clinical anchor | Role in this study |
|---|---:|---|---|---|

The key principle is that every dataset has one defined job. Do not let secondary datasets appear to be interchangeable.

State clearly that 124 or 125 task-level/model rows do not equal 124 or 125 independent participants. The independent primary Parkinson sample is 62 people.

### 2.3 Control-reference score

Explain the eight inputs and the healthy-reference logic before showing the equation.

The reader should understand the score in one plain-language sentence first:

> The score measures how far a person's multivariate gait pattern lies from the gait pattern expected for a healthy person of similar age and height while accounting for covariance among the gait features.

Then give the mathematical definition and define every term.

Emphasize that Parkinsonian severity was not used to fit the healthy reference.

### 2.4 Primary severity association

State:

- outcome;
- model family;
- covariates;
- participant clustering;
- multiplicity correction;
- standardized effect reporting;
- prespecified primary comparison.

Explain why gait speed is a critical comparator. It is a strong, familiar summary measure and a potential confounder or simpler explanation for many gait associations.

### 2.5 Healthy-reference robustness

Explain the 2,000 resamples as a test of whether the score's severity signal depends heavily on a particular set of healthy controls.

Do not describe this as external replication.

### 2.6 Incremental information beyond speed

Make participant grouping impossible to miss.

Explain why records from one participant must remain together in the same fold. This is both a statistical safeguard and a credibility point.

Report ranking and absolute prediction as different targets:

- Spearman: relative ordering;
- Pearson: linear association;
- RMSE and MAE: numerical error;
- calibration: agreement of predicted and observed scale.

### 2.7 Analytical reconstruction

Explain why reconstructing the eight inputs from raw walkway records is a prerequisite before applying the score longitudinally.

Give the prespecified agreement gates and the number of matched trials. Explain ICC and concordance coefficient in a sentence rather than assuming every reader uses them routinely.

### 2.8 Long-interval stability

State before the analysis that the repeated visits are at least six months apart and therefore cannot estimate short-term test-retest repeatability.

Define the stability criterion before reporting the result.

This ordering matters. A reader should see that the failure threshold was not invented after observing the ICCs.

### 2.9 External evidence

Keep CARE-PD, Mobilise-D, Mendeley, and one-site sensitivity analyses in a clearly labeled secondary section.

State what each can and cannot test. If the measurement representation cannot reconstruct the frozen score, call that test not estimable rather than failed replication.

## 3. Results

### Rule for every Results subsection

Use this order:

1. answer the scientific question in the first sentence;
2. give the quantitative evidence;
3. identify the figure or table;
4. give one restrained interpretation sentence if needed.

Do not make the reader dig through setup before discovering what happened.

### 3.1 Conventional feature screen

Keep this short. Its purpose is to establish why association alone is insufficient and motivate the control-reference score.

The important observation is not a leaderboard of 11 features. It is that several measures can associate with severity while failing other requirements such as speed independence or repeatability.

### 3.2 Primary score association

Lead with the effect:

- standardized association: 0.301;
- 95% CI: 0.089 to 0.513;
- q=0.0055.

Then report the speed-adjusted effect:

- standardized association: 0.279;
- 95% CI: 0.083 to 0.475;
- q=0.0053.

Explain only what these numbers establish: concurrent severity association beyond a simple speed adjustment in the frozen primary model.

### 3.3 Healthy-reference robustness

Lead with the strongest fact:

- all 2,000 control-reference resamples retained the same association direction;
- median standardized effect: 0.291.

Do not call 2,000 resamples 2,000 replications. They test dependence on the reference sample.

### 3.4 Added value beyond speed

This subsection needs a figure that shows the distribution across repeated validations, not only one median.

Primary participant-level result:

- median delta Spearman: +0.0474;
- ranking improved in 98% of repetitions.

Then immediately show the boundary:

- RMSE improved in 66% of repetitions;
- MAE improved in 44%;
- better exact-score prediction was not established.

This contrast is one of the report's strongest credibility signals. Do not compress it into a generic claim that "performance improved."

### 3.5 Analytical agreement

State that all eight reconstructed inputs passed the prespecified analytical-equivalence gates across 252 matched trials.

Use one compact figure or table rather than eight separate plots unless a diagnostic plot reveals something important.

### 3.6 Six-plus-month stability

Treat this as a major result, not an appendix note.

Report:

- SelfPace ICC(A,1)=0.647, 95% BCa CI 0.439 to 0.863, n=45;
- HurriedPace ICC(A,1)=0.282, 95% BCa CI -0.009 to 0.543, n=44;
- both missed the prespecified lower-confidence-bound criterion.

Then state the boundary: because visits were separated by at least six months, these estimates mix measurement variation with possible disease, medication, and health-state change. They do not estimate short-term repeatability.

### 3.7 Evidence boundaries and secondary datasets

Use a summary table rather than several pages of secondary analyses.

Recommended columns:

| Evidence question | Status | What the data support | What they do not support |
|---|---|---|---|

This is where the report can show scientific maturity by explicitly labeling unsupported layers.

## 4. Discussion

### Opening paragraph: answer the question

The first Discussion paragraph should state the whole answer without making the reader wait.

The logic is:

- the score was associated with concurrent gait-specific severity;
- the association was not driven by one healthy reference sample;
- relative severity ranking improved beyond speed;
- exact-score prediction did not consistently improve;
- the eight inputs could be reconstructed analytically;
- the frozen score failed the six-plus-month stability criterion.

Then give the larger meaning: association, analytical validity, prediction, longitudinal behavior, and transport are separate properties.

### 4.1 Why the positive results matter

Explain why the ranking result is scientifically interesting without pretending it establishes a clinical tool.

A useful question to answer is: what information does the multivariate score capture that gait speed alone does not?

Stay at the evidence level. The study supports additional ordering information, not a diagnosis or an exact severity predictor.

### 4.2 Why the negative result matters

This should be one of the strongest paragraphs in the paper.

Do not apologize for the failed stability criterion. Explain what it teaches.

A weaker paper would have stopped after the significant association. This study continued to a harder validation layer and found that the same candidate did not satisfy it. That result narrows the claim and identifies the next necessary experiment.

The tone should be confident but not self-congratulatory. Scientific credibility comes from keeping the failure visible.

### 4.3 Position against prior work

Organize the literature by question, not by author.

Suggested sequence:

1. prior studies showing gait-severity relationships;
2. studies validating sensor or walkway measurement systems;
3. studies of short-interval reliability;
4. longitudinal digital mobility studies;
5. multicenter or external-validation work.

Then explain what this project combines and what it still does not solve.

Do not write a paragraph that lists six papers one after another. Synthesize them into the scientific argument.

### 4.4 Limitations

A strong limitations section increases trust when it is specific.

Address at least:

- 62 independent participants in the primary severity analysis;
- repeated tasks do not create additional independent people;
- gait-specific clinical rating rather than total disease burden;
- long-interval visits cannot isolate measurement error from real change;
- no verified matched clinical anchor for responsiveness;
- limited one-site transport sensitivity;
- no validated eight-input bridge for CARE-PD;
- secondary datasets use different outcomes and measurement representations;
- no diagnostic threshold or prospective clinical-use test.

For each limitation, say what consequence it has for interpretation. Do not merely list it.

### 4.5 Next experiment

End the Discussion with the experiment that would most efficiently reduce uncertainty:

1. same device and task;
2. short repeat interval;
3. medication timing documented;
4. health and clinical state documented;
5. gait-specific clinical rating at every session;
6. frozen score, no target-outcome refitting;
7. independent-site bridge validation after short-term reliability is established.

This shows the reader that the project generated a concrete scientific next step rather than only a conclusion.

## 5. Conclusion

Keep the conclusion short.

It should answer three questions:

1. What was established?
2. What failed or remains unresolved?
3. Why does that distinction matter to the field?

Do not introduce new datasets, new statistics, or new claims here.

The final sentence should widen the lens from this score to digital biomarker validation generally, but it must remain supported by the study.

## Figure plan

Figures are part of the argument. A reader who studies only the figures and captions should still understand the scientific progression.

### Figure 1: Study design and validation ladder

**Question answered:** What was tested, and in what order?

Show development and validation stages with clear dataset boundaries. Avoid version numbers unless essential.

### Figure 2: Primary association and healthy-reference robustness

**Question answered:** Is the score associated with severity, and does that result depend on a particular healthy-control reference sample?

Combine the primary estimate with the resampling distribution if readable.

### Figure 3: Added value beyond gait speed

**Question answered:** Does the score add information beyond speed, and what kind of information?

Show participant-level distributions of delta Spearman and at least one absolute-error metric. The visual should make the ranking-versus-prediction distinction obvious.

### Figure 4: Analytical reconstruction

**Question answered:** Can the eight score inputs be recovered from raw walkway records with prespecified agreement?

A compact forest/table hybrid is preferable to eight disconnected panels.

### Figure 5: Six-plus-month stability

**Question answered:** Did the score meet the prespecified long-interval stability criterion?

Show ICC estimates, confidence intervals, and the threshold on the same axis. The failure should be visually obvious without exaggeration.

### Figure 6: Evidence summary

**Question answered:** Which validation layers are supported, incomplete, failed, or not estimable?

This is the final synthesis figure. Keep the categories literal and define them in the caption.

### Caption rule

Every caption should contain:

- the scientific takeaway in its first sentence;
- sample size or unit of analysis;
- essential statistical definition;
- abbreviation definitions;
- enough context to interpret the figure without searching several pages backward.

Avoid captions that say only what colors or symbols appear.

## Writing techniques that make the report compelling without hype

### 1. Use scientific tension, not advertising language

A compelling paper contains an unresolved question the reader wants answered.

For this study, that question is stronger than a generic "Can AI help Parkinson disease?" frame:

> If a gait score correlates with severity, does it still look useful after the harder tests required for biomarker qualification?

That question creates genuine tension because the answer is not guaranteed.

### 2. Put the gap in one sentence

If the reader cannot underline one sentence that names the missing evidence, the Introduction is not finished.

### 3. Give every paragraph one job

A useful paragraph pattern is:

`context -> specific claim -> evidence -> consequence`

Do not repeat the same idea in new words for emphasis.

### 4. Put important information where readers naturally emphasize it

Keep subjects and verbs close. Put the consequential result near the end of a sentence when possible.

Weak:

> In 100 repetitions using five-fold validation grouped at the participant level and several metrics, an improvement was observed for the Spearman correlation.

Stronger structure:

> In 100 participant-grouped repeated validations, the extended model improved severity ranking in 98% of repetitions.

The number and conclusion land together.

### 5. Move from old information to new information

Start a sentence from the concept the previous sentence established, then add the new result. This makes technical prose feel inevitable rather than abrupt.

### 6. Use active, concrete verbs

Prefer:

- measured
- compared
- retained
- improved
- failed
- passed
- estimated
- reconstructed

Use fewer vague verbs such as "demonstrated," "highlighted," or "showcased" when a more exact verb exists.

### 7. Let numbers carry emphasis

Do not call a result "highly robust" when the actual evidence is more persuasive:

> All 2,000 reference resamples retained the same association direction.

### 8. Translate technical meaning immediately

After a difficult statistic, tell the reader what question it answers.

Example pattern:

`metric -> numerical result -> plain-language meaning -> boundary`

### 9. Build credibility by showing the boundary next to the success

When reporting the 98% ranking improvement, state nearby that RMSE and MAE did not improve consistently. This prevents the reader from wondering whether an inconvenient result was hidden.

### 10. Use emotion through consequence

Do not say the work is "life-changing" or "groundbreaking."

Explain the consequence:

- objective gait measures could make assessment more scalable;
- an unstable measure cannot automatically be trusted to monitor one individual;
- a failed qualification layer can prevent a promising association from being overinterpreted.

The reader supplies the emotional weight.

## Natural-writing rules

The final prose should sound like a student researcher who understands the work deeply, not like a template.

### Prefer

- simple precise words;
- varied but controlled sentence length;
- direct subject-verb construction;
- concrete nouns and verbs;
- explicit uncertainty;
- occasional short sentences when a result truly deserves emphasis;
- transitions that describe the actual logical relationship.

### Avoid

- repeated uses of "crucial," "robust," "novel," "groundbreaking," "comprehensive," "remarkable," "significant" when the statistic can speak for itself;
- "delve," "underscore," "showcase," "pivotal," "multifaceted," and other generic prestige vocabulary;
- a paragraph beginning with "Furthermore" every few lines;
- identical sentence lengths;
- repeated three-item rhetorical lists for rhythm alone;
- slogans such as "not just X, but Y";
- dramatic claims that the study will transform patient care;
- unexplained acronyms;
- software-version language in the scientific narrative;
- excessive bolding or decorative headings.

If a sentence could appear unchanged in 1,000 unrelated research papers, rewrite it around the actual experiment.

## Build order

Do not draft from the Introduction downward.

### Phase 1: Lock the evidence

1. Freeze the claim-evidence table.
2. Verify every number against the repository outputs.
3. Decide which analyses are primary, secondary, exploratory, failed, and not estimable.
4. Lock terminology for the score, outcome, tasks, and evidence layers.

### Phase 2: Build figures first

1. Create the six-figure narrative.
2. Write captions that state the takeaway.
3. Check that the figures alone tell the study's progression.
4. Remove any figure that does not change the reader's understanding.

### Phase 3: Write Methods and Results

1. Methods should follow the analysis sequence.
2. Results should follow the figures.
3. Keep interpretation minimal in Results.
4. Verify denominators every time participant counts and row counts differ.

### Phase 4: Write the Discussion

1. Start from the central answer.
2. Explain the positive evidence.
3. Explain the failed stability layer.
4. Compare with the literature by question.
5. State limitations and their consequences.
6. End with the next experiment.

### Phase 5: Write the Introduction last

Once the final evidence and interpretation are fixed, write only the background necessary to understand them.

Use the four-paragraph funnel:

`neurological stakes -> existing digital gait evidence -> validation gap -> present study`

### Phase 6: Write the conclusion and title

The conclusion should not exceed what the Discussion established. The title should name the real contribution, not the most marketable possible claim.

### Phase 7: Line edit for natural prose

For every paragraph ask:

- What is this paragraph doing?
- Can its first sentence say that sooner?
- Is any sentence merely repeating the previous sentence?
- Is there a simpler precise word?
- Is the most important information in an emphatic position?
- Does the prose sound like the person who performed and understands the work?

### Phase 8: Scientific integrity pass

Search specifically for these terms and inspect every occurrence:

`diagnose`, `progression`, `treatment`, `clinical utility`, `validated`, `external validation`, `reliable`, `generalizable`, `biomarker`, `prediction`

Make sure each use matches the evidence.

## Reviewer tests before the report is finished

### The 30-second test

After the title, first paragraph, and first figure, can a reader state:

- the neurological problem;
- the validation gap;
- what the study did;
- why it matters?

### The two-minute skim test

Read only:

- title;
- last paragraph of Introduction;
- figure titles and captions;
- first paragraph of Discussion;
- conclusion.

The full scientific story should still be recoverable.

### The skeptical neurologist test

Can the report explain:

- why gait is neurologically relevant;
- what the clinical anchor represents;
- why the work does not diagnose Parkinson disease;
- why six-month stability is not short-term repeatability?

### The skeptical statistician test

Can the report explain:

- why N=62 is the independent primary sample;
- why participant grouping prevents leakage;
- why Spearman and RMSE answer different questions;
- what the confidence intervals and q-values mean;
- which tests were prespecified?

### The external-validation test

Can a reader identify exactly which dataset is a true measurement-compatible test and which external datasets provide only related evidence?

### The integrity test

Ask a hostile but fair question:

> What result in this study most weakens the strongest possible claim?

The report should answer that question itself before a reviewer asks it.

### The memory test

Twenty-four hours after reading, the ideal reader should remember something close to:

> The score looked promising across several validation layers, but failed the long-interval stability test. The study shows why association alone is not enough for a longitudinal digital biomarker.

If that is not the remembered idea, the report's emphasis needs revision.
